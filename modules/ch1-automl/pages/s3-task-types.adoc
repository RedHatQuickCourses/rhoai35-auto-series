= The Four Prediction Task Types
// :page-audio-src: s3-task-types.wav
// :page-transcript-src: s3-task-types.txt

Objective::

Match any predictive business problem to one of the four AutoML task types and its default optimization metric.

---

Here's the good news: no matter how complicated the business problem sounds, every predictive question you'll ever hand to AutoML boils down to one of just *four* task types. Learn to spot which one you're looking at and you're most of the way there.

Even better, you often don't have to decide alone. When you load a CSV into the Red Hat OpenShift AI dashboard, it inspects your columns, infers their types, and *recommends* a task type and a sensible default metric. You should understand the four tasks well enough to know when it's right.

== Turn the business problem into a data question

Every project starts with a business problem: someone wants to know who's going to leave, how much something will cost, what next quarter's demand looks like. That's always your starting point. The catch is that the platform can't act on an intention, it can only act on columns. So the habit to build early is translating that business question into a data question, and that translation almost always comes down to *one* column: the *target*, the value you want the model to predict for new rows.

When a stakeholder says "I want to know who's going to leave us," your next move is to open the CSV and find the column that records who actually left, then look hard at what's in it.

Two quick questions answer almost everything:

. *What kind of value lives in the target column?* Is it a handful of labels, or a number that can slide up and down a scale?
. *Is time part of the story?* Is each row anchored to a date or timestamp, with something identifying which sequence it belongs to?

Answer these and the task type falls out on its own. That's the skill this section builds, so that when the dashboard recommends a task type, you can confirm it rather than take it on faith.

== The quick decision tree

The clue is almost always in your *target column*, the thing you're trying to predict:

[source]
----
Raw CSV Data
│
├── Target has 2 unique values ─────────► Binary Classification    ("Yes / No?")
├── Target has 3+ text categories ──────► Multiclass Classification ("Pick 1 from a menu")
├── Target is continuous numbers ───────► Regression               ("How much?")
└── Target tied to Timestamp + ID ──────► Time Series Forecasting  ("What's next over time?")
----

Let's take them one at a time.

== 1. Binary classification: "Yes or No?"

The workhorse of enterprise ML. Your target column has exactly *two* values, 0/1, True/False, Churn/No-Churn.

* *Data cue:* two unique values in the target.
* *You'll see it in:* customer churn prevention, fraud detection, credit-risk approval.
* *Default metric:* *Accuracy*, but switch to *ROC-AUC* or *F1* when the classes are lopsided. *ROC-AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve)* measures how well the model separates the two classes across every decision threshold, on a 0.5-to-1.0 scale where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect separation. On a dataset that's 99% legitimate and 1% fraud, a model that guesses "not fraud" every time is 99% accurate and completely useless, so accuracy alone can be misleading.

== 2. Multiclass classification: "Pick 1 from a menu"

Same idea as binary, but now there are three or more categories to choose from.

* *Data cue:* three or more distinct text labels in the target.
* *You'll see it in:* support-ticket routing, customer segmentation, classifying *which type* of equipment failure occurred.
* *Default metric:* *Accuracy*.

== 3. Regression: "How much?"

When the answer isn't a category at all but a number on a continuous scale.

* *Data cue:* the target holds continuous numeric values, with no time component.
* *You'll see it in:* price estimation, predicting a patient's medical charges, estimating how long an operation will take.
* *Default metric:* *R^2^ (R-squared)*, the share of variance the model explains, on a 0.0-to-1.0 scale. Closer to 1.0 is better.

== 4. Time series forecasting: "What's next over time?"

Regression's time-aware cousin. You're not just predicting a number, you're predicting it *forward*, respecting the order of events.

* *Data cue:* the dataset has a *timestamp* column, an *ID* column identifying each sequence (say, each store or product), and a numeric *target*.
* *You'll see it in:* retail inventory demand, quarterly sales projections, capacity planning.
* *Default metric:* *MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error)*, it grades the forecast against a naive "tomorrow looks like today" baseline, so you can tell whether the model is genuinely adding value or just going through the motions.
