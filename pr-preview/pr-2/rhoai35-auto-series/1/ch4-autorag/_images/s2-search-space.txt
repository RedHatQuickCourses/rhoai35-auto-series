In this section, we'll walk the Auto RAG search space, and learn to read the three core metrics it uses to score your configurations.

Once you kick off a run, Auto RAG systematically tries combinations of retrieval settings, scores each against your evaluation set, and ranks them. Two things are worth understanding: what it's varying, and how to read the scores that come back.

Let's start with the search space. Think of every setting as a dial. Tuning them by hand means testing one combination, waiting, adjusting, and repeating. Auto RAG filters them for you.

It varies your chunking strategy, how documents are split: recursive chunking versus hybrid structural chunking, where the structural option preserves headings and table structure and kicks in with the Better Quality preset. It varies chunk size and overlap, how big each piece is and how much neighboring pieces share. It tries contextual retrieval, where each chunk is enriched with document-level context before it's embedded and indexed, instead of being stored as a bare fragment; this one also activates with the Better Quality preset, and Auto RAG weighs it against standard recursive chunking to see if it genuinely improves your results. It compares search modes, pure vector, or semantic, search against a hybrid approach that blends semantic and keyword matching. It optimizes top-k, how many chunks to retrieve per question. And it experiments with different embedding and foundation models.

On those models, a hard limit: you can select up to two embedding models and three foundation models per run. Stay within that cap, because going over it causes the run to fail. And a tip: the recommended default embedding model is BGE-M3. It's versatile, supports over 100 languages, does both dense and sparse retrieval, and runs with low memory overhead.

A common question is how Auto RAG handles different languages. You don't declare a language up front. The system detects the language of your evaluation questions automatically, and it handles mixed-language corpora gracefully. So you can point it at German, Spanish, or Japanese documents and score them directly, with no upfront translation to English. One caveat: auto-detection only helps if the models you picked actually support those languages. Check the Model Catalog to confirm your chosen foundation and embedding models cover the languages in your documents.

Now the metric triad. Auto RAG scores every candidate on three complementary metrics, and understanding them is the key to good diagnostics. First, Answer Faithfulness, a hallucination check: did the answer stick to the retrieved context, or did the model make things up? Second, Answer Correctness, a ground-truth check: did the answer match the known-good answers in your evaluation set? Third, Context Correctness, a retrieval check: did the system fetch the right document chunks in the first place?

The reason there are three, not one, is that together they isolate exactly where a pattern succeeds or fails.

So let's read the combinations. When two metrics disagree, the pattern is telling you something specific. High faithfulness with low correctness means the model answered faithfully from what it retrieved, but the retrieved context didn't actually contain the true answer; suspect your chunking strategy, or a mismatch in your evaluation dataset. High correctness with low faithfulness means the answer was right, but the model pulled it from its own training memory rather than your documents; it got lucky this time, and on unseen questions that's a hallucination risk. And low context correctness with high answer correctness means retrieval fetched irrelevant chunks but a strong foundation model compensated; it looks fine now, but it's fragile, and it'll break on query types the model hasn't effectively memorized.

A final piece of advice: if you're new to evaluating RAG, don't chase a single top score. A configuration where all three metrics are solid is far more trustworthy in production than one with a dazzling correctness score propped up by weak retrieval. Consistency beats a lucky headline number, the same lesson the Auto M L report card taught.

Now that you can read the leaderboard and understand why a pattern ranks where it does, you're ready for the last step: turning that winning pattern into an application-ready configuration, through inspection and export.
