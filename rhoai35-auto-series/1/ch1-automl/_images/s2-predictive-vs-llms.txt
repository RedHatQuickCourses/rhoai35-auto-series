In this lesson, we will learn how to position predictive models against large language models, specifically comparing their costs and performance behaviors. We will also define exactly what kind of data makes for a good AutoML candidate.

Walk into almost any room today, and the conversation is focused on large language models. While LLMs are undisputedly transformational, there is a trap in that excitement. Many organizations are reaching for massive, GPU-hungry models to answer questions that a tiny, deterministic model could answer better, cheaper, and a thousand times faster.

Let’s look at a side-by-side comparison of predictive models versus large language models to understand why.

Predictive models, typically used in AutoML and tabular data scenarios, are designed for narrow, single-task structured decisions, such as predicting churn, fraud, or pricing. In contrast, LLMs are built for broad, open-domain reasoning and text generation.

When it comes to data input, predictive models handle low-dimensional structured data—think of a spreadsheet with dozens or hundreds of features. LLMs, however, process high-dimensional, unstructured text across billions of tokens.

In terms of scale, predictive model artifacts are lightweight, ranging from kilobytes to a few megabytes. LLMs require massive infrastructure, often spanning tens to hundreds of gigabytes. Because of this, predictive models can run on standard CPU infrastructure, while LLMs require high-VRAM GPU clusters. 

Performance is also a major differentiator. Predictive models offer microsecond inference for high-throughput systems, whereas LLMs generally operate with second-level response times. Finally, predictive models provide deterministic logic with clear feature importance, making them highly auditable. LLMs rely on probabilistic reasoning, which requires additional guardrails.

In short, predictive models are cheap, fast, and deterministic. They can process millions of rows on the hardware you already own, at a fraction of the cost of a GPU fleet, and they provide a clear, repeatable paper trail for every decision.

Now, you might be wondering: is your data a good candidate for AutoML? While AutoML is powerful, it is not magic. It expects a specific shape of data. Before you start a project, ensure your dataset meets these four criteria:

First, it must be in a tabular structure. This means your data consists of rows as entities and columns as attributes, stored in a CSV format. Use UTF-8 encoding with a header row. You can upload up to 32 megabytes through the dashboard, or ingest up to 100 megabytes directly from Amazon S3.

Second, ensure you have a clear target label. This is the single column that holds the ground-truth answer you want to predict for new data.

Third, look for feature diversity. Your dataset should have a healthy mix of both numeric and categorical columns.

Fourth, consider the volume. You need at least 500 to 1,000 rows to create a prototype, though 10,000 rows or more is the standard for a serious enterprise evaluation.

It is just as important to know when to put the AutoML tool down and reach for something else. Do not use AutoML for unstructured media, such as images, audio, or video—those require specialized deep-learning pipelines. Likewise, if your goal is open-ended text generation, like building a chatbot or performing document synthesis, that is the domain of LLMs. Finally, if your problem hinges on complex, hand-crafted business rules that a model cannot infer from a raw table, AutoML is likely not the right solution.

The key takeaway is to match the tool to the shape of the problem. When you do that, AutoML feels like a superpower. Force it onto the wrong problem, and it will only lead to frustration.
