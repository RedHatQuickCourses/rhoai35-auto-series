Across this course, you've picked up three powerful capabilities: Auto M L for predictive modeling, Auto RAG for grounded generation, and prompt management for governing your models. On their own, each solves a specific problem. Together, they add up to a complete business process. To see how they fit, let's follow a single decision from raw data all the way to a human action.

Picture a loan desk. A loan officer is reviewing an application, and to decide well they need to answer two different questions.

First, how risky is this applicant? That's a classic Auto M L use case. You feed your historical loan data into the system, it evaluates models like XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, and neural networks, and it produces a ranked leaderboard. Promote the winning model to a serving runtime on KServe, and you can pull a precise risk score from a REST API in microseconds.

Second, why that score, and what does policy require? A raw number isn't enough for compliance. The officer needs a formal credit memo, something like: this applicant is in the elevated-risk band; per section four of our policy, applications above 250,000 dollars require a secondary review. That's where Auto RAG comes in. It retrieves the relevant policy chunks and drafts a memo grounded in your company's actual documents, rather than whatever the model might have memorized in training.

Underneath both is prompt management. The system instructions that set the tone and the requirements of that credit memo are versioned and stored in a registry backed by MLflow. That gives you a clear audit trail, so you can prove exactly which prompt version generated a given memo when compliance asks.

The pattern is this: the platform handles the tedious heavy lifting, predictive AI for the numbers and generative AI for the language, so the human officer can spend their expertise where it matters, making the final judgment.

Building the workflow is one skill; knowing how to troubleshoot it is what separates a user from an operator. When results look off, diagnose each stage on its own.

For your Auto M L model, lean on the report card. Don't trust raw accuracy on imbalanced data; look at the confusion matrix and the ROC and precision-recall curves. And check feature importance, to be sure the model isn't leaning on irrelevant or biased data.

For Auto RAG, use the metric triad from earlier. Don't read the scores in isolation; look for the pattern between them. High faithfulness with low correctness tells you the model answered honestly from its context, but it was handed the wrong information, so fix your retrieval or chunking, not the model. High correctness with low faithfulness suggests the model got lucky from its own memory rather than your documents, a dangerous trend that won't hold on unseen data.

For prompt management, your diagnostic tool is the audit trail. When an answer is wrong, check the lineage in the registry to see which version ran, compare it against earlier versions, and roll back if you need to.

Always identify the failing stage before you start changing settings. A low score is just a symptom; the pattern is the diagnosis.

It's just as important to know when these automated capabilities are the right tool. Stick with them when your problem involves predictable, structured data; when your GenAI app simply needs access to your specific facts; or when the base model already has the core skill, like summarization, but lacks your internal knowledge.

Think about escalating beyond automation only when you hit a skill gap that better prompting or retrieval can't close. If a model keeps failing to hold a required tone or output format no matter what context you give it, that may be a sign you need to fine-tune a specialized model. Just remember fine-tuning is heavier and more expensive, so reach for it only after you've exhausted the other options.
