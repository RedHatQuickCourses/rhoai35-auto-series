The Search Space and the Metric Triad

Objective

Understand the configurations AutoRAG searches and how to interpret the three metrics it scores them on.

This is the heart of AutoRAG. Once you kick off a run, it systematically tries combinations of retrieval settings, scores each against your evaluation set, and ranks them. Two things are worth understanding here: what it’s varying, and how to read the scores that come back.

What AutoRAG searches

Think of every setting below as a dial. Tuning them by hand means testing one combination, waiting, adjusting, and repeating for weeks. AutoRAG sweeps them for you:

  • Chunking strategy, how documents are split. Recursive chunking versus hybrid structural chunking (the latter preserves headings and table structure, and kicks in with the Better Quality preset).

  • Chunk size and overlap, how big each piece is and how much neighboring pieces share.

  • Search mode, pure vector (semantic) search versus hybrid search that blends semantic and keyword matching.

  • Top-k, how many chunks to retrieve for each question.

  • Embedding model, the model that turns text into vectors. You can select up to 2 embedding models per run.

  • Foundation model, the LLM that writes the final answer. Up to 3 foundation models per run.

Stay within 3 foundation models and 2 embedding models per run. Going over that cap causes the run to fail, so it’s a limit to respect, not push.
The recommended default embedding model is BAAI/bge-m3. It supports over 100 languages, does both dense and sparse retrieval, and runs with low memory overhead, which is why it shows up throughout the AutoRAG material.

A quick word on languages

AutoRAG automatically detects the language of your evaluation questions, so you don’t declare it up front, and it handles mixed-language corpora gracefully. One caveat worth remembering: auto-detection only helps if the models you picked actually support those languages. Check the Model Catalog to confirm your chosen foundation and embedding models cover your document languages.

Reading the metric triad

AutoRAG scores every candidate on three complementary metrics. You met these by name in the course overview; here’s what each one actually tells you:

  • Answer Faithfulness, a hallucination check. Did the answer stick to the retrieved context, or did the model make things up?

  • Answer Correctness, a ground-truth check. Did the answer match the known-good answers in your evaluation set?

  • Context Correctness, a retrieval check. Did the system fetch the right document chunks in the first place?

The reason there are three, not one, is that they isolate where a pattern succeeds or fails. And the real skill, the AutoRAG equivalent of reading a confusion matrix in chapter 2, is interpreting them in combination.

Diagnosing the combinations

This little decision guide is the most practical thing on the page. When two metrics disagree, the pattern is telling you something specific:

  • High Faithfulness + Low Correctness: the model answered faithfully from what it retrieved, but the retrieved context didn’t actually contain the true answer. Suspect your chunking or a mismatch in your evaluation dataset.

  • High Correctness + Low Faithfulness: the answer was right, but the model pulled it from its own training memory rather than your documents. It got lucky this time; on unseen questions it’s a hallucination risk.

  • Low Context Correctness + High Answer Correctness: retrieval fetched irrelevant chunks, but a strong foundation model compensated. It looks fine now but is fragile, it will break on query types it hasn’t effectively memorized.

New to evaluating RAG? Don’t chase a single top score. A pattern where all three metrics are solid is far more trustworthy in production than one with a dazzling Answer Correctness propped up by weak retrieval. Consistency beats a lucky headline number, the same lesson the AutoML report card taught.

What’s next

You can now read the leaderboard and understand why a pattern ranks where it does. The last step is turning a winning pattern into something your application can actually call, which is where inspection and export come in.
